<a id="project"></a>
## Project overview — ActiveEdge

**ActiveEdge** is a sustainable performance sportswear concept developed around activewear for fitness-conscious South Africans. The original team charter describes the intended offering as performance activewear made from recycled and eco-certified materials, with products such as leggings, sports bras, T-shirts, shorts and outerwear.

The live project website presents the brand, product collections, best-selling products, fabric information, delivery and returns information, and a shopping interface. The live site labels itself as a **demo store with no real payments**; this portfolio therefore treats it as a demonstration product experience, not as evidence of completed live payment processing.

## My individual contribution

I joined the ActiveEdge project as an additional team member after the original team charter had already been prepared. My contribution was focused on the product's digital experience and technical implementation, spanning design and development work.

### UX/UI design and wireframing

I was responsible for developing the website wireframes, working from **low-fidelity to mid-fidelity**. This involved translating the product concept into page structures and interface layouts, considering the placement of content, navigation and the way a visitor would move through the website. I also contributed to product design decisions to help shape a consistent website experience for the ActiveEdge brand.

### Frontend development

I contributed to the frontend code by implementing and refining interface elements and touching up existing frontend work. This connected the design stage to the working website and required attention to the visual presentation and consistency of the user interface.

### Backend and database configuration

I worked with **Supabase** on the backend, including setting up the database and configuring columns. This gave me practical exposure to the data layer supporting the application and to the relationship between the information stored in the backend and the experience presented through the frontend.

<div class="reflection">

  <p>Because I joined ActiveEdge after the original charter had been prepared, I used it as a reference point for understanding the team's purpose and agreed way of working rather than claiming to have co-created it. Joining after the original charter also made me aware of the importance of onboarding: an additional member needs to understand the team's objectives, communication norms and current responsibilities before contributing effectively. In future projects, I would make that onboarding process explicit and ensure that the team has a shared understanding of any role changes.</p>
  <p><strong>Development insight:</strong> The charter gave me context for contributing within an existing team structure. It reinforced that technical work is more effective when it is aligned with shared goals, clear communication and accountability.</p>

<div class="reflection">

  <p>My work on the ActiveEdge wireframes required me to think about the website from the visitor's perspective before focusing on the implementation. Moving from low-fidelity to mid-fidelity wireframes helped turn a broad product idea into more considered page structures and interface layouts. This process highlighted that decisions about navigation, content hierarchy and product presentation affect how easily a visitor can understand and use a website.</p>

This gave me a practical connection between a design decision and the development work needed to represent it in the interface. Working on the Supabase database setup also made it important to consider the information that the application needs to store and how the backend data structure relates to what the user sees.</p>

  <p><strong>Development insight:</strong> User stories help keep design and implementation focused on the people using the product. My experience reinforced the value of connecting user needs, interface decisions and technical tasks instead of treating them as separate activities.</p>

<div class="reflection">
  <h3>Reflection — Product Backlog</h3>
  <p>The ActiveEdge work helped me understand how a product backlog can connect the overall product direction to the individual activities needed to build it. My own contribution covered several related work areas: wireframing, product design, frontend implementation and Supabase database configuration. Looking at these activities together makes it clear that a website is not developed through visual design alone; the interface and the underlying data structure also need to support the product experience.</p>
  <p>The backlog is useful because it gives the team one place to understand what work is required and why it matters. For example, wireframes help establish the structure of the experience, frontend tasks turn that structure into an interface, and database tasks support the information the application needs to work with. ### Individual reflection

<div class="reflection">
  <h3>Reflection — Sprint Backlog</h3>
  <p>My contribution to ActiveEdge involved work that moved between design and implementation. This made the purpose of sprint planning particularly clear to me: a broad product requirement needs to be translated into smaller tasks that can be understood, assigned, tracked and reviewed within a defined period. Wireframing, product design, frontend refinement and backend database configuration are related, but each involves different activities and evidence of completion.</p>
  <p>A sprint backlog can help the team make those activities manageable. For design work, a task can produce a specific wireframe or design revision. For frontend work, it can identify the interface element that needs to be implemented or refined. For backend work, it can describe the required database configuration and the expected structure. Breaking the work down in this way makes progress easier to communicate and reduces ambiguity between people working on connected parts of the product.</p>
  <p>This project also reinforced the importance of handovers and dependencies. A frontend change may rely on an agreed design, while the way information is displayed may depend on the fields configured in the database. The sprint plan should make these relationships visible so that work can be completed in a sensible order and issues can be raised early.</p>
  <p>For future sprints, I would make sure every selected task has a clear owner, a definition of done and a realistic scope. I would also review the board during the sprint rather than relying only on an end-of-sprint status. The actual sprint goal, dates and selected cards should accompany this reflection so that the evidence shows how the team's plan was applied in practice.</p>
  <p><strong>Development insight:</strong> Sprint planning helps turn a broad product backlog into a focused, time-bound plan. It is especially valuable when design, frontend and backend activities need to coordinate towards one product outcome.</p>
</div>
